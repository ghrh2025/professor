Week 4.2 · The Buying Center
One purchase. Six jobs.
When a business buys something expensive, no single person decides. Chapter 7 names six jobs that get done inside that purchase. Here they are in plain English:
1InitiatorStarts it. Names the need out loud first.
2UserWill actually use the thing after it is bought.
3InfluencerShapes what counts as a good answer. May hold no seat at all.
4GatekeeperControls what gets through. Cannot approve, can stop.
5BuyerHandles the terms and the paperwork.
6DeciderSigns. Owns the number afterward.
Two things to hold onto. A job is not a person. One person can do two of these, and one job can be done by two people. And the two who can stop it are not the one who can start it.

Dr. Priya Nandakumar
Urologist. Operates on the da Vinci Xi most Wednesdays. She put the request in writing after the FDA cleared Medtronic's Hugo for urology on December 3, 2025. The machine she is asking for is the machine she will spend her own Wednesdays on.
Dr. Wade Ellery
About 1,400 robotic operations, nearly all on a da Vinci. Does hernias and gallbladders, which Hugo is not cleared for. He is not on the capital committee. Several of the twelve credentialed surgeons trained under him, and when he says a platform behaves unpredictably in a bleeding field, the room hears a fact.
Marcy Doyle
Manages the operating rooms at Gateway. Has offered no opinion on either machine, only a constraint: a second platform means a second set of instrument trays, a second sterile-processing workflow, and a second turnover routine. She can staff one platform well or two platforms badly.
Terrance Bell
Runs biomedical engineering, and he is why the item keeps getting tabled. He will not sign off on equipment his own team is not permitted to service. Coverage runs $80,000 to $190,000 a year per machine, and those agreements have historically been voidable if an outside vendor touches the equipment.
Dana Kessler
Negotiates the contracts. Least emotional stake in the room and the worst news. She can negotiate the price of a console. She has never once been able to negotiate the instruments, and about 85% of what Intuitive collects comes from instruments, not robots.
Mark Boone
Chairs the capital committee and has $4.1 million to allocate this year across the system. A robotic case runs about $3,300 in direct cost, more than $2,000 above the same operation done laparoscopically, and no payer reimburses the difference. He has to defend the number to the board in September.
